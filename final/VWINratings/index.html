pH (“potential for Hydrogen") is the concentration of hydrogen ions (H+) in water. The higher the concentration of H+, the lower the pH. The lower the concentration of H+, the higher the pH. The is because pH is the negative logarithm of the H+ concentration. Because pH is logarithmic, a change of 1 pH unit means the H+ concentration changes by a factor of 10. A pH of 7 is considered a neutral pH. Water with a pH less than 7 is described as “acidic,” while water with a pH greater than 7 is described as “basic” or “alkaline.”

Turbidity is caused by fine particles of solid material that are suspended in water. “Turbid” water can also be described as “cloudy” or “opaque.” Turbidity in stream water is typically caused by suspended soil and dust particles in runoff that washes into the stream following a rain or snowmelt. Turbidity in lake water, by contrast, is typically caused by the growth of microscopic algae near the surface. Turbidity is measured by shining a beam of light on a sample of water and determining the intensity of the light that is scattered at a 90º angle. A common measure of turbidity is the Nephelometric Turbidity Unit (NTU). A higher NTU value indicates that the water is cloudier and light cannot penetrate as easily.

Suspended solids, especially fine soil particles in runoff, can endanger aquatic life by coating the stream bottom and clogging the gills of small fish and insects, causing suffocation. Suspended solids also absorb heat from the sun, raising the temperature of the surrounding water and causing thermal stress. Sources of suspended solids include construction sites, eroding stream banks, suburban parking lots, agricultural fields and city streets. Suspended solids are measured by drying and weighing a filter, passing the water sample through the filter in order to trap suspended solids on it, then drying and weighing the filter again. The difference between the “before” and “after” weight of the filter gives the concentration of suspended solids.

Alkalinity is the capacity of water to resist a drop in pH when mixed with acids (aka its "buffering capacity"). Alkalinity is measured by adding sulfuric acid, H2SO4, to a known volume of water until the pH decreases to 4.3. The more H2SO4 required to lower the pH to 4.3, the greater the alkalinity of the water. High alkalinity is often caused by underground limestone formations or industrial waste discharges.

The two major nutrients driving the growth of algae and other aquatic plants are nitrogren and phosphorus. The chemical form of phosphorus that is taken up by plants as a nutrient from the environment is orthophosphate, PO4. Orthophosphate is incorporated into DNA, ATP, and other molecules essential to life. Soil erosion and agricultural runoff are main contributors of phosphorus to a body of water. If there is too much in the water it can speed up eutrophication (excessive amounts of nutrients cause low levels of dissolved oxygen in the water).

Nitrate, NO3, is the form of nitrogen that plants take up from the environment as a nutrient. Nitrate may come from underground minerals that dissolve in groundwater as it flows to streams and lakes. Nitrate may also come from man-made fertilizers that are applied, for example, to agricultural fields, golf courses, and suburban lawns and enter streams and lakes in runoff when it rains.

Ammonia, NH3, is the major form of nitrogen present in animal and human waste. Ammonia is toxic to aquatic life. One of the principal functions of sewage treatment plants is to remove ammonia from wastewater before it is discharged to streams and lakes.

The intestines of humans and wildlife are home to large numbers of different kinds of bacteria. High fecal coliform presence indicates that the water is contaminated by fecal matter and poses risk of infection. Fecal coliforms are a class of bacteria containing E. coli. To measure fecal coliform, water samples are collected in sterilized containers, filtered, and incubated at a specific temperature overnight. The colonies that form during incubation are counted and recorded as the number of colony forming units per 100 mL of water. A count of 200 colonies (bacteria) per 100 ml is considered to be the upper limit of acceptable risk for contact recreation such as swimming.

The total phosphorus test measures all the phosphorus in a water sample, including soluble reactive phosphorus (dissolved or “free” phosphorus), phosphorus attached to soil particles, phosphorus in organic waste, and phosphorus in microorganisms such as algae.

Conductivity, also called specific conductance, measures the capacity of water to conduct an electrical current. It depends on the total concentration of positively and negatively charged molecules, or ions, that are dissolved in the water. Generally, ions come from underground minerals that dissolve in groundwater on its way to a stream. Because the water in runoff is generally “soft” water from rain or snowmelt, the conductivity of stream water usually decreases as the volume of water contributed by runoff to stream flow increases.
